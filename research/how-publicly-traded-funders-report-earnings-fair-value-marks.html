Marking a Lawsuit to Model: How Publicly Traded Funders Report Earnings Before the Cash Arrives

The US Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit reversed the $16.1 billion YPF judgment against Argentina on 27 March 2026. Burford Capital, by portfolio size the largest publicly traded litigation funder, had backed the plaintiffs. Its next quarterly report cut the value of its stake from about $1.7 billion to $93 million. Both figures are Burford-only: they leave out the outside investors' interests that its consolidated accounts include. Burford said the write-down cost it no cash.

It cost no cash because the value that disappeared had never come in. Burford's share of the gains on the case had been reported as income over several years without arriving as cash. Separately, it had collected $236 million, Burford-only, in 2016 to 2019 by selling slices of the claim. An unrealized gain is reported income, not cash received.

A hypothetical case, every figure invented, shows how such a value is built. A funder puts $10 million into a lawsuit. If the claimant wins, the funder receives $24.2 million in two years. If not, it receives nothing. The funder judges the chance of winning at 50 percent and discounts future money at 10 percent a year. Half of $24.2 million is $12.1 million. Two years of discounting divides that by 1.21. The value is $10 million, the same as cost, so no gain is reported.

A year later the claimant wins at trial. The funder puts the chance at 80 percent, with payment one year off. Eighty percent of $24.2 million is $19.36 million. Divided by 1.10, that is $17.6 million. The funder reports a gain of $7.6 million. No cash has arrived.

Then an appeals court reverses. The funder cuts the chance to 10 percent and expects two more years of proceedings. Ten percent of $24.2 million is $2.42 million. Divided by 1.21, that is $2 million. The funder reports a loss of $15.6 million. No cash has left. After the first $10 million, cash moves only at the end: $24.2 million if the claimant finally wins, nothing if not. Over the life of the case, reported income must equal the cash received less the $10 million paid. The valuations decide only when it is reported.

Each of those values is called a mark, and the measure is fair value. IFRS 13 and its US counterpart, ASC 820, define it as the price that would be received to sell an asset in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date. It is not management's preferred number or a forecast of what a case will pay. Pending lawsuits rarely trade, and the odds and timing of a result cannot be observed, so the inputs sit at Level 3 of the standards' hierarchy.

Those standards say how fair value is measured. Whether a case is carried at fair value at all turns mostly on what the funding contract is, and classifying one takes judgment. Under IFRS, the first question is whether the contract is a financial asset. Omni Bridgeway says its direct funding contracts give it no unconditional right to receive cash, and it carries them as intangible assets at cost, subject to impairment. For a financial asset, IFRS 9 looks at the business model in which it is held and at whether its cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest. One whose cash flows fail that test must be measured at fair value, with changes booked in profit or loss, apart from an option for some equity investments. Omni says its participation interests in its funds fail that test.

Burford, under US GAAP, takes a different route. It says its single case, portfolio and legal risk management assets meet the definition of a derivative and are accounted for at fair value. Those were about three quarters of its consolidated capital provision assets at the end of 2025. It has elected fair value for its equity-method investments, and the assets of the funds it consolidates are carried at fair value too. Three publicly traded funders show the range.

A real mark can also move without a ruling. In the third quarter of 2026, a rise of about 90 basis points in the average discount rate produced $94 million of fair value losses, Burford-only. A ruling can move it much further. Burford's policy calls for a partial write-down after a loss in an intermediate appeals court. For the nine months to 30 September 2026, its fair value loss on the YPF-related assets was $1.607 billion, Burford-only. The case is not over: the plaintiffs petitioned the Supreme Court on 30 September 2026.

Jay Newman, who led Elliott Management's fifteen-year pursuit of Argentina's defaulted bonds, set out the case against marking in a Financial Times Alphaville column on 14 October 2026. In his account, a mark looks like cash in the earnings line and turns lumpy case results into smooth profit. The mark then feeds bonuses, dividends, the share price and borrowing. His remedy is to carry claims at cost, write them down early, book gains when cash arrives and publish the estimated portfolio value outside the accounts.

That argument is his. Burford's filings let a reader check three of the channels he names (bonuses, dividends and borrowing), and they do not work alike.

Compensation accruals follow the marks, and cash payments wait. Burford records long-term incentive compensation as the related fair value gains are recognized, and the accrual can be reversed when fair value declines. The compensation is not paid until the gains have been realized in cash. For the first nine months of 2026, the consolidated accounts show $122.6 million of the accrual reversed because of the YPF ruling and $14.8 million paid.

Dividends are a decision of the board, subject to a solvency test under Guernsey law. Burford does not intend to declare an interim dividend for 2026, and says it will weigh future dividends as its portfolio matures and its leverage comes down.

Borrowing capacity is the channel Burford itself flagged in advance. Its bond indentures permit some new debt by reference to its debt-to-equity ratio, and its statement of 27 March 2026 warned that a material write-down could take equity below the level required. Equity attributable to Burford's shareholders was $848 million at 30 September 2026, against $2.448 billion at the end of 2025, and debt was $2.3 billion, all the same on both bases. Cash and marketable securities were $514 million Burford-only and $531 million consolidated. The quarterly report says the ratio now impairs Burford's use of some baskets, the exceptions in its indentures that permit new debt and payments such as dividends. It adds that other baskets, not tied to the ratio, remain available.

The industry has its answers. For a financial asset whose cash flows fail IFRS 9's test the mark is required, not chosen, and Burford's filings treat fair value as following from the derivative classification. The valuation firm Stout wrote in June 2025 that holding a case at cost leaves out the time value of money. And a mark can be tested against later cash. Omni Bridgeway's fair value conversion ratio, published outside its audited accounts, divides cash from completed cases, net of new spending, by the fair value last reported for them. It was 103 percent for the year to June 2025 and 105 percent for the year after.

That test needs care. A mark is a discounted, probability-weighted value. A fair comparison with later cash adjusts for money put in after the mark, for timing and discounting, and for the same ownership share and fees on both sides. It counts the losses, the partial realizations and the cases still unresolved. Omni's ratio uses the last fair value reported before completion, when much of the uncertainty may already be resolved. An older mark is the sterner test.

Litigation Capital Management, an Australian funder quoted on London's AIM market, published a blunter comparison in its annual report for the year to 30 June 2025. In figures for LCM alone, the investments realized that year had been held at a cumulative fair value of A$76.6 million, 2.8 times the cash invested. They realized 1.8 times. LCM cited a share of losses much higher than its long-term average as a key reason. The next year's report did not repeat the comparison.

One adverse outcome does not by itself show that an earlier probability-weighted value was unreasonable. That holds for YPF. One favorable outcome proves as little. The test is the record across a portfolio.

LCM also shows what happens when losses meet a shortage of cash. Its annual report for the year to 30 June 2026 gives figures for LCM alone, leaving out the outside funds that its statutory accounts consolidate. On that basis the loss before tax was A$173.6 million, cash generated from concluded investments was A$2.4 million, and cash at year end was A$2.1 million, against net debt of A$123.4 million. LCM announced an orderly run-off on 30 September 2026: existing cases run to conclusion, there are no new investments, and cash goes first to its lender, funds managed by Northleaf Capital Partners. Its accounts disclose a material uncertainty about whether it can continue as a going concern.

For a claimant, that makes the funder's own finances part of the bargain, and the questions are about liquidity. Does the money for my case come from the funder's own cash, a fund it manages or borrowing? Is my whole budget available today, or does it wait on other cases paying out? What does the agreement say if the funder is sold, goes into run-off or stops paying? The Library has a piece about funders that withdrew mid-case.

For an investor, the questions are about the valuations and their record. What chance of success, timing and discount rate stand behind the marks? How much of the period's income is unrealized? When cases concluded, how did the cash compare with the value carried a year or two earlier, losses included? Is each figure consolidated or the company's own share? The cash line belongs beside the income line: Burford's cash receipts, a Burford-only measure, were $336 million in the first nine months of 2026, against $423 million a year earlier.

None of this says marks are wrong or cost is right, and it is not legal or investment advice. In the hypothetical, a $7.6 million gain and a $15.6 million loss passed through the accounts, and neither moved a dollar. The March write-down cost Burford no cash for the same plain reason: the gains it erased had brought none in.
