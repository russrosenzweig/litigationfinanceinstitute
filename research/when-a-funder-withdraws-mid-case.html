When a Funder Withdraws Mid-Case: Three Australian Class Actions That Ended Without a Decision

Funded cases are supposed to end with a verdict, a settlement, or a loss the funder absorbs. Three recent Australian class actions ended another way: the funder stopped paying and the case stopped with it. Two Federal Court of Australia judgments and one set of news reports show what a court looks at when that happens, what happens to the people in the group, and what anyone considering funding should ask before signing. These are three examples, not proof of a trend, and Australian class action procedure differs from American practice.

The most recent is Buttgieg v Macquarie Bank Limited [2026] FCA 1500. Justice Derrington gave the applicant leave to discontinue on 1 October 2026, and the reasons were published in amended form on 9 October. The court listed four problems. The applicant did not appear to fit the group definition, which exposed the case to a strike-out risk. She believed the group might hold no more than thirteen people, and fewer than seven after opt-outs. Many potential members may already have been repaid by the bank. And the funder had terminated its arrangement after judging the case too risky, with no alternative funding in sight.

On funding, the judgment is direct. The court said the absence of funding materially impeded continuing the case, and noted that the funder's decision may reflect doubts about the claims' prospects. That is a useful reminder that a funder's exit is itself information. It is not only a financing event.

The consequences for the group were mild because the case ended early. There was no order as to costs, and group members will not be liable for any costs. The court ordered no further notice to potential members, reasoning that the group was very small and unidentified, the case had limited publicity, and the bank had never been served. Limitation periods were suspended from the start of the case under section 33ZE(1) of the Federal Court of Australia Act, so the court found that discontinuance created no limitation problem, while noting some uncertainty in the authorities about when time starts running again.

A June 2026 decision shows the harder version. In Inger v State of Queensland [2026] FCA 792, Justice Hespe approved discontinuance of a class action challenging Queensland's additional foreign acquirer duty and land tax surcharge. After the High Court's decision in G Global 120E T2 Pty Ltd v Commissioner of State Revenue [2025] HCA 39, handed down on 15 October 2025, the funder (referred to in the notice as CASL) no longer considered the case commercially viable. The lead lawyer advised there was no reasonable prospect of another funder stepping in, and the court accepted evidence to that effect. The respondents agreed to no order as to costs, and earlier costs orders were vacated. This judgment is older than this article's usual window, but it shows the other side of the coin: here the court ordered that limitation periods for group members begin running again 30 days after the date by which the applicants must file their notice of discontinuance, and the notice told members to seek urgent legal advice.

The third example rests on news reports because the court's reasons were not located. ABC News reported on 8 October 2026 that two Queensland child safety class actions in the Federal Court were being discontinued after the funder, identified as Therium, decided in September 2025 it no longer wanted to fund them. It reported that Justice Dowling noted the lawyers had made significant attempts to find alternative funding, and that the parties had to prepare a notice to group members. National Indigenous Times reported on 16 October 2026 that the case had been formally discontinued, that group members are not barred from suing again and are not liable for costs, and that $350,000 of the State's legal costs was to be paid by the funder rather than the lead applicants. The two outlets name slightly different Therium entities, and the costs detail comes from a single report, so treat it as unconfirmed until the order is published.

What ties these together is the test the Federal Court applies: whether discontinuance would be unfair, unreasonable or adverse to group members' interests. That test protects people through notice, costs and limitation periods. It does not restore a lost claim or a lost year. The practical differences between the cases were stark: no notice and a suspended clock in one, a restarted clock and an urgent-advice warning in another, and in the third a group member quoted as unsure she has the strength to start again.

For someone seeking or evaluating funding, the lesson is to read the exit terms as carefully as the price. Ask your lawyer whether the funding agreement lets the funder stop, on what grounds, and at what milestones. Ask who pays adverse costs if the funder withdraws, and whether the funder's obligation survives its exit, as the reported $350,000 arrangement suggests it can. Ask how much time remains on any limitation period and what happens to it if the case ends. And ask whether the case could realistically find a second funder, because in all three matters the answer appeared to be no. None of this is legal advice, and a lawyer who knows the jurisdiction should answer these questions for your own matter.
